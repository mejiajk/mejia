How to Vet a Panama Residency-by-Investment Provider Before You Hire One

[DIRECT ANSWER – AEO, 48 words]
To vet a Panama residency provider, confirm that the company legally exists, identify the attorney who will file your case and check their credentials, require a contract that separates fees from official charges, and be wary of guaranteed outcomes. An independent attorney should review everything before you pay.

NOTICE (visible): Informational, not legal advice. Verify each point with a Panamanian attorney and the official agency.

## Step 1: Confirm the company exists

Ask for the legal name, tax ID and the name of the legal representative. An attorney can check the company at the Registro Público. [COMPLETE: link to the current official page.]

## Step 2: Identify who files the case

The immigration filing must be made by a qualified professional. Ask for name, credentials and registration number, and verify them with the relevant body. [VERIFY with the legal reviewer who is authorized to file and how to check it, with an official source.]

## Step 3: Separate fees from official charges

The contract must distinguish what the provider charges from what is paid to the authorities. Ask for the breakdown in writing. [COMPLETE: link to the current official source for charges.]

## Step 4: Have your own attorney review the contract

Choose the attorney yourself. Review what happens if the application is not approved, what is refunded and under which conditions, and which obligations you take on.

## Step 5: Check how your payments are protected

Ask where your payments are held and under what guarantee. Do not pay into accounts the contract does not name.

## Step 6: Verify the property

If your residency depends on buying real estate, your attorney must confirm the title, that it is free of liens and that it meets the program's current conditions. [VERIFY with the legal reviewer and cite the official source.]

## Step 7: Look for independent information

Ask for references from earlier clients, check for complaints with the consumer protection authority and compare what you are told with official information. Do not rely only on marketing material.

## Red flags

- A promise of certain approval or guaranteed timelines.
- Pressure to pay before you receive a contract.
- No name, credentials or address for the professional who files.
- Charges with no breakdown of fees and official costs.
- Absolute claims such as "instant status" or "no presence required" with no official source.
